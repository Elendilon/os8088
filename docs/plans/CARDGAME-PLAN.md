# CARDGAME-PLAN.md — a two-player turn-based card duel for os8088

**STATUS: PLAN, REVISION 1. Nothing is built.** Every number below that is not
marked *measured* is an **estimate**, and §3.6 is the bench that has to run
before the renderer's shape is chosen. The name, the faction names and several
rules are deliberately left as **options for the owner to pick** (§1, §16).

This file is the design record. When work lands, SPEC.md **§97** becomes the
binding contract and this file moves to `docs/plans/completed/`.

---

## 0. The ask, restated

A two-player turn-based card battle game, patterned loosely on a Flash-era
board duel (the reference screenshot: an isometric board, two facing
territories, unit counts `4/10` and `5/10`, a gold counter, `INCOME THIS TURN`,
and a heart per player).

| | |
|---|---|
| players | 2 — hot-seat, AI, or networked |
| network | two os8088 machines over Ethernet; **and** two machines on a direct null-modem cable (serial or parallel) |
| solo | an AI opponent, and a **campaign** — a map of AI duels that earns cards |
| deck | built from cards you own, **one faction per deck**, three factions at launch, room for more |
| board | **4 columns × 5 rows** — rear/front per player, 10 characters a side |
| resources | **GOLD** (generated by characters, mostly in the rear) and **SOULS** (earned when an enemy dies, by its power rating) |
| presentation | windowed **or** fullscreen; animated front menu with custom graphical buttons |
| animation | **23 animated features** at once — 20 characters, 2 player bases, 1 moused-over card |
| framerate | **18 fps, held, always** |
| disk | **350KB for the whole game**, because it ships on a 360KB floppy of its own |
| heap | must run in **~400KB of free heap**, holding at most two factions' art |

## 2. The envelope — the arithmetic everything else obeys

This section is first on purpose. **The framerate requirement and the
23-animated-features requirement together are the hardest constraint in the
brief, and they decide the art format, the sprite size and the animation
rate.** Every later section is downstream of these numbers.

### 2.1 The frame

| | |
|---|---|
| system tick | **18.2065 Hz** → **54.925 ms** a frame |
| the target machine | IBM PC/XT, **8088 at 4.77 MHz** → ~262,000 cycles a frame |
| any `gfx_*` arrival, small call | **~756 µs** *measured*, and ~20% less since §5.7 — see the warning below |
| `OSAPI_GFX_BLIT1`, 128×128 band | **12,588 µs** *measured* (PERFORMANCE.md Set 77) |
| ...so a 1bpp band costs | **6.15 µs a byte**, **0.77 µs a pixel** *derived from the above* |
| `OSAPI_GFX_BLIT4`, VGA planar decoder | **106.9 cycles a pixel** = **22.4 µs a pixel** *measured* |
| `OSAPI_GFX_BLITP`, four planes | *"the bytes and nothing else"* — **estimate ~3.1 µs a pixel**, 4× the band. **NOT MEASURED. §3.6 measures it.** |
| RAM `rep stosw` | **1.76 µs a byte** *measured* |
| RAM byte read-modify-write | **15.3 µs a byte** *measured* |
| a full task switch | 693 µs *measured* |

> **Do not quote 756 µs as a floor a design must beat.** PERFORMANCE.md Part 2
> says so in as many words: it is the fixed part of a *small* call, `gfx_hline`
> is 678 µs for 6 pixels and 862 for 312, and Set 89 is the worked example of a
> design argued from the aggregate and got wrong. The number this plan actually
> leans on is the **per-byte band cost**, because our renderer is one blit a
> sprite.

### 2.2 The finding that decides the art format

Three ways to put a 64×80 character sprite on a VGA:

| route | bytes moved | cost *(est. from the rates above)* | 23 features, one update each |
|---|---|---|---|
| `GFX_BLIT4` — packed 4bpp, kernel decodes | — | 5,120 px × 22.4 µs = **114.7 ms** | **2.6 seconds.** Unusable. |
| `GFX_BLITP` — 4 planes, pre-composed | 2,560 | **~15.7 ms** | **361 ms** → 2.8 full rounds a second |
| `GFX_BLIT1` + pen — 1bpp band, two colours | 640 | **~3.94 ms** | **90.6 ms** → 11 full rounds a second |

**`gfx_blit4` is out.** A single character would be two whole frames. It is the
right slot for a picture drawn once and the wrong one for anything that moves —
which is exactly §5.4.1.3's own account of why the planar decoder exists and
what it still costs.

**16-colour sprites cost 4× a 1bpp band, because 16 colours *is* four bits a
pixel and the VGA framebuffer is the wall.** There is no trick that avoids it:
`gfx_blitp` is already a bare `rep movsb` per plane per row with zero
transpose, so it is the floor for full colour and the floor is four times the
one-bit floor.

### 2.3 …so the animation rate is a budget, and here is the table

Take **40% of the frame** for idle animation (22 ms), leaving 60% for the HUD,
the hand, input, the AI's background thinking and slack. With 23 features:

| format | cell | bytes | per commit | commits/frame at 40% | **fps per feature** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1bpp band | 48×64 | 384 | 2.36 ms | 9.3 | **7.4** |
| 1bpp band | **64×80** | **640** | **3.94 ms** | **5.6** | **4.4** |
| 1bpp band | 80×96 | 960 | 5.90 ms | 3.7 | **2.9** |
| 4 planes | 48×64 | 1,536 | 9.45 ms | 2.3 | **1.8** |
| 4 planes | 64×80 | 2,560 | 15.7 ms | 1.4 | **1.1** |

**The owner's own instinct is what the arithmetic says**: *"our characters each
have slower animations to support keeping the framerate up while having a
larger sprite each."* At 64×80 and 1bpp a character breathes at **4.4 fps** —
a 4-frame ping-pong idle (A B C B) is a **0.9-second cycle**, which reads as a
slow breath rather than a stutter, and 18 fps of *board* is never at risk
because the pacing wheel (§3.4) is a fixed budget rather than a hope.

**A 386 is a different machine and gets a different answer.** `OSAPI_CPU_INFO`
answers `CPU_8086 / CPU_286 / CPU_386`, which is a *fact the code can test*
rather than a guess about speed (PERFORMANCE.md rule 7). §3.3 turns that into
a **Detail** setting with a measured default.

### 2.4 The adapters

| adapter | screen | what the board gets |
|---|---|---|
| VGA | 640×480, 16 colours | the design target. 4×5 cells at 64×80 = 256×400, plus HUD and hand |
| Hercules | 720×348, 1bpp | wider, much shorter. Cells ~72×56. Workable |
| CGA | **640×200**, 1bpp | **the problem.** 200 rows total. Five rows of characters, a hand and a HUD do not fit at any sprite size worth drawing |

**CGA is an open question and §16 carries it.** The three candidate answers:
a compact schematic board with 24-pixel-tall characters; a scrolling board
showing three rows at a time; or the game refuses CGA in its own words
(§47 rule: grey a fact, never a guess — and *"this game needs more than 200
rows"* is a fact). DOT DELIRIUM's precedent is **size from the surface**
(§93.3) and be honestly smaller on the smaller adapter; that is the default
assumption here until somebody looks at a 24-pixel character on a real CGA.

**A 1bpp adapter reads on one bit and the pen is NOT READ there** (§5.4.2.2).
So the faction colours are a VGA feature and the mono adapters need the art to
read as *silhouette plus interior line*, the way §93.2.1's maze does. That is
an art-direction constraint, not a code one, and it is cheapest to honour at
the drawing board.

### 2.5 Disk and heap

**The disk is 354 clusters of 1,024 bytes** — a 360KB floppy's real capacity —
and the brief's 350KB is therefore the whole volume bar a rounding. Every
package and data file on a shipped disk is **lz4-compressed** by default
(`PKGZ ?= lz4`, §20.13), so what a byte count must be measured against is the
*compressed* size; pixel art compresses well and code does not.

A first sketch, and it has room:

| | uncompressed | on disk (est. ×0.6) |
|---|---|---|
| the package primary (`.O88`) | ~48KB | ~30KB |
| character sprites — 48 characters × 4 frames × 640 B | 123KB | ~60KB |
| card faces — 48 × art + frame | ~30KB | ~15KB |
| player bases — 3 factions × 6 frames | 27KB | ~14KB |
| board grounds — 3 terrains | 60KB | ~25KB |
| menu art, buttons, title | ~40KB | ~20KB |
| campaign map | ~30KB | ~15KB |
| **total** | | **~179KB of 354** |

That leaves about half the disk for more frames, more cards, a fourth faction
or 16-colour static art. **The disk is not the binding constraint. The frame
is.**

**Heap**: at most two factions are live in a match, so only their art is
resident. 2 factions × 16 characters × 4 frames × 640 B = **82KB** of sprites,
plus the board ground, the HUD, the hand and the composition scratch. Call it
**150KB** against ~400KB free. Comfortable — and it is comfortable *because*
the sprites are 1bpp. The four-plane variant is 328KB of sprites alone and
does not fit beside anything.

### 2.6 What this game is not

- **Not a `kern_small` package.** It wants ~150KB of heap and a 128KB machine
  has ~50KB. It goes in `$(SMALLOMIT)` with a stated reason, and `smallreq`
  is the gate that keeps it there (§24.5).
- **Not an `fsx` bracket with a foreign mode.** It is turn-based and wants the
  kernel's cursor, the event ladder and the file API. §11.2's fullscreen
  *surface* — a real window covering the screen — is what "fullscreen" means
  here, and **windowed and fullscreen are one renderer at two sizes** (§3.1).
  A foreign mode would mean two art pipelines for no gain.

## 3. The renderer

### 3.1 One surface, sized from the content box

`DOT DELIRIUM` is the precedent and its three consequences are ours (§93):
it is **bigger on a Hercules than on a CGA**, it draws **one blit an actor a
frame and nothing else**, and it **re-measures the surface** rather than
assuming one.

- The window is created at a preferred size per adapter (`OSAPI_WM_PREFER`
  takes exactly that — three (w, h) pairs in VGA / HERC / CGA order).
- `OSAPI_WM_OWNBG` is set: we paint every pixel of our content, so the
  kernel's white fill in front of `W_PAINT` is skipped. Without it every
  repaint flashes white first — PERFORMANCE.md rule 2's canonical violation.
- `OSAPI_WM_NOANIM` — the zoom-open on a 400-pixel window is a second of
  nothing.
- Fullscreen is `OSAPI_FULLSCREEN` (AL=1, BX = our window). §11.2.1 binds the
  keys: **F** toggles, **Esc** leaves. The layout recomputes from the new
  content box, the sprite masters are re-cut, and nothing else changes.
- `OSAPI_WM_ONRESIZE` re-runs the layout when the content box changes and we
  did not ask.

**Layout is a table computed once per size change**, never per frame: cell
size, the 20 cell origins, the hand slots, the HUD rects, the pen table. A
resize is not a thing that happens inside a frame (§93.5's own words).

### 3.2 One blit a feature, and the ground is baked into it

**The rule, and it is the whole renderer**: a character is put down as **one
opaque band that includes its own ground**, so drawing it where it used to be
*is* the erase. There is no instant at which a sprite is off the glass, no
second call to be pre-empted between, and no erase pass at all.

This is §79.5.1's finding and §93.5.1's. The screen saver's first draft blanked
the box and then drew six primitives into it; the field reported *"the fish
flicker"*, because 4.5 ms with the sprite absent is not a brightness dip, it is
a strobe. **We do not get to relearn this.**

Two consequences follow, and they are design constraints rather than code:

1. **The four combat columns share ONE ground texture.** If a cell's ground
   depended on which column it was in, a sprite frame would need one variant
   per column and the art multiplies by four. In the reference screenshot the
   middle of the board is a single sand strip and the *outer* columns are the
   players' own terrain — so the fiction already agrees with the arithmetic.
   The players' territory, banners and base are outside the 4×5 grid.
2. **A band is cut on the CELL grid, not the byte grid.** `gfx_blit1` only
   asks for a multiple of 8, and a band cut there reaches part-way into the
   next cell, so whatever is drawn there is composed partly — which on the
   glass is a thing that changes shape when a neighbour animates and changes
   back. §93.5.1 paid for that one. Cells are a multiple of 8 wide by
   construction, so cutting on the cell grid costs nothing.

### 3.3 Colour — the pen, and the strip trick

`OSAPI_GFX_BLIT1_PEN` (slot 0x04A8) says what a set bit and a clear bit become:
**two colours a band, in one pass, for free** (§5.4.2.2 — measured at +0.62% on
a 128×128 band, a fixed ~78 µs). On a 1bpp adapter the pen is *ignored rather
than refused*, so the same code runs everywhere.

Two colours a character is thin beside the reference art. Three ways out, in
increasing cost:

- **(a) One pen a character.** Each card carries its own (ink, paper) pair, so
  twenty characters on the board are twenty two-tone sprites in up to twenty
  colour pairs. Cheapest; reads as a colourful board made of flat figures.
- **(b) Horizontal strips, one pen each.** The sprite is blitted as 2–3
  stacked bands — head, body, base — each with its own pen. **The bytes are
  identical** (each strip is part of the same sprite); what is added is one
  arrival per extra strip. At 3 strips and 5.6 commits a frame that is ~11
  extra arrivals a frame; at the *measured* small-call floor that is ~8 ms, and
  §3.6 must price it rather than this paragraph. Buys 4–6 colours a character
  at close to 1bpp cost. **This is the interesting one.**
- **(c) Four planes** via `gfx_blitp`, full 16 colours, at 4× the bytes. §2.3
  says that is ~1.1 fps a feature on an 8088 and perfectly affordable on a 386.

**Proposal: a `Detail` setting with three arms — `Flat` (a), `Banded` (b),
`Rich` (c) — defaulted from `OSAPI_CPU_INFO` and the adapter**, and the default
chosen by §3.6's bench rather than by this document. The art pipeline emits all
three from one master (§4), so the setting costs disk and not drawing time.

### 3.4 The pacing wheel — how 23 features animate at a fixed rate

**A fixed budget, not a hope.** The renderer holds a list of the 23 animated
features and a **credit budget in bytes per frame**, set at layout time from
the measured per-byte cost and the frame share. Each frame:

1. Walk the wheel from where it stopped.
2. For each feature whose animation clock says it is due, commit its band and
   subtract its byte cost from the frame's credit.
3. Stop when the credit is spent. **Remember the position.** Next frame starts
   there, so no feature can be starved by the ones in front of it.

What this buys:

- **A consistent animation speed**, because a feature's clock advances on the
  system tick and not on how many neighbours drew. A feature that misses its
  slot is *late by one frame*, not slowed down.
- **Overrun is impossible by construction.** The credit is the contract.
- **Graceful degradation**: on a slower surface the wheel simply turns more
  slowly and every feature slows together, which is what an eye forgives. The
  failure mode it removes is the one that is not forgivable — some characters
  animating and others visibly hitching.
- **The attack phase takes the whole credit** (§3.5), by handing the wheel a
  different budget for the duration.

The idle clock is deliberately **not** a free-running counter per feature: all
features of one kind share a phase table with a per-feature offset, so twenty
characters do not all inhale on the same tick. (Twenty synchronised idles read
as a single pulsing blob; staggered ones read as a crowd.)

### 3.5 The attack phase

The brief: **attacks resolve simultaneously from both sides, ≤1 second a row,
and the animation may focus solely on the attack.**

So the attack phase suspends the idle wheel and spends the whole frame budget
on the row being resolved: at most four attacking characters, their
projectiles, the impacts and the floating damage numbers. Five rows × ≤18
frames = **≤5 seconds** for a full combat phase, and a **Fast Combat** setting
(§6.9) halves it for players who have seen it enough.

**Simultaneity is a resolution rule, not an animation one.** Both sides'
damage for a row is computed from the state *at the start of that row*, then
applied together — so two units that would kill each other both die. The
animation plays them together; the arithmetic never interleaves.

Rows resolve **top to bottom**, which is arbitrary but must be pinned, because
deaths in row 0 change nothing about row 1 (damage was already computed for the
row being resolved and rows do not interact) — *unless* a card says otherwise,
and §7's splash/volley effects are exactly such cards. Where they exist, they
read the state at the start of **their own row's** resolution.

### 3.6 WAVE 0 — the bench that decides all of the above

**Nothing in §3.2–§3.5 is settled until this has run**, and it is the first
thing built: a `tests/` package (never shipped, §19.10's rule) that puts up
23 bands of a configurable size and format and reports guest cycles a frame
off MartyPC's own counter.

Rows it must answer:

| row | question |
|---|---|
| `blit1` 48×64, 64×80, 80×96 | the per-byte band cost on **this** build — §2.1's 6.15 µs is derived from a 2026 measurement of a 128×128 band and must be confirmed at our sizes |
| `blit1` + pen, same sizes | what the pen's fixed ~78 µs really is at a small band |
| `blit1` ×3 strips | §3.3(b)'s extra arrivals, priced rather than estimated |
| `blitp` 48×64, 64×80 | the four-plane floor — **this number does not exist anywhere in the tree** |
| all three, Hercules and CGA | the 1bpp adapters are not a scaled VGA |
| 23 features, the full wheel | the whole-frame figure, which is the only one that answers the brief |

**Measured on MartyPC** — a cycle-accurate 4.77 MHz 8088, the default
instrument — and the numbers go in a dated `docs/reports/` file, because a
measurement is true of the tree it was taken on and of no other.

It also settles a question nothing else can: **whether 64×80 is the cell, or
whether the cell has to come down.**

## 4. Assets — one master, three outputs

### 4.1 The pipeline

Art is authored as PNG at a **single master size per kind** and a host-side
tool (`tools/os88cards.py`, in `os88logo.py`'s and `cp437font.py`'s shape)
emits the shipped blobs. The tool is a build input like any other, and
committing generated output is this tree's convention where the generator is
slow or needs a dependency the build does not have.

```
art/char/<faction>/<card>_<frame>.png     ->  CARDS.DAT   (sprite bank)
art/card/<faction>/<card>.png             ->  FACES.DAT   (card faces)
art/base/<faction>_<frame>.png            ->  BASES.DAT
art/board/<terrain>.png                   ->  BOARD.DAT
art/menu/*.png                            ->  MENU.DAT
art/map/*.png                             ->  MAP.DAT
```

From one master the tool emits, per record: the **1bpp band** (threshold plus
an authored dither for the mono adapters), the **pen pair** or the **strip
table**, and — if `Rich` survives §3.6 — the **four-plane block**. Every record
is stored at the master size and **cut to the live cell at layout time by
nearest neighbour**, which for the sizes this game uses is a copy or "take
every other bit" — §93.5's approach exactly, and its lesson comes with it:

> **A scaled row is a fixed number of bytes whatever the cell is**, so a narrow
> image is a wide one with an empty tail. `dotdel` shipped a cutter that packed
> one byte a row while the composer strode two, and on a CGA every actor was
> noise. One constant, used by the writer and the reader.

### 4.2 Where the bytes live at run time

`apps/os88parts.inc` (§20.12) is the mechanism: named, sized parts inside the
one `.O88`, claimed and loaded on demand, optionally compressed on disk
(`OP_COMP`) and optionally lazy (`OP_LAZY`).

| part | flags | when |
|---|---|---|
| `MENU` | `OP_COMP`, `OP_LAZY` | at the front menu; dropped when a match starts |
| `BOARD` | `OP_COMP` | at match start |
| `FACTION` ×3 | `OP_COMP`, `OP_LAZY` | **only the two in play** — this is the line that makes 400KB work |
| `MAP` | `OP_COMP`, `OP_LAZY` | campaign only |

**Why parts and not sidecar files**: `apps/c64`'s ROMs were a sidecar until
§20.12 wave 6, and *"a copy that took the program and left it behind was a
machine that could not start."* One file is one file.

**The load path is the regression to watch.** PAINT-1BPP-PLAN §42.25 records a
decoder that shipped at 199 cycles a pixel against the packed arm's 49 — the
*load* half was the real cost, not the draw half. Ours reads a part and expands
it; both halves get a number in §3.6's report.

### 4.3 The heap, and the one rule that bites

Every claim is born **pinned** (§66). The package's own region and every art
claim must declare `OS88_REGION_MOVABLE` with a relocation **proc**, and
`tests/unit/t_movable.py` fails the build otherwise — the way out is a line in
`tests/movable.txt` with a reason, and that list only turns one way.

**And a region declaration alone is inert if the package hires a worker**:
§66.6.2's frame pins the region however it is declared, so the worker needs
`OS88_WORKER_RESTARTABLE` at the spawn. We do hire one (§11.3), so both are
owed.

Before declaring, ask what segment words we hold that point inside ourselves.
For this package that is the **sprite bank index** — if the index stores
segments rather than offsets-from-a-base, the proc has real work; if it stores
offsets, the proc is a `ret`. **Store offsets.**

## 5. The game — the rules

### 5.1 The board

```
        P1 REAR   P1 FRONT  |  P2 FRONT   P2 REAR
row 0  [       ] [       ]  |  [       ] [       ]
row 1  [       ] [       ]  |  [       ] [       ]
row 2  [       ] [       ]  |  [       ] [       ]      <- P1 base    P2 base ->
row 3  [       ] [       ]  |  [       ] [       ]
row 4  [       ] [       ]  |  [       ] [       ]
```

- **4 columns × 5 rows.** Column 0 = P1 rear, 1 = P1 front, 2 = P2 front,
  3 = P2 rear. **A "row" is a lane** and runs across all four columns.
- **10 characters a side, maximum** — 2 columns × 5 rows, which is where the
  reference screenshot's `4/10` comes from.
- The players' **bases** sit outside the grid, one each side, and are two of
  the 23 animated features.

### 5.2 Resources

| | earned | spent on |
|---|---|---|
| **GOLD** | a base income each round, plus every surviving character's `GOLD` stat at end of round (mostly a **rear** ability) | playing characters |
| **SOULS** | when an enemy character dies: **the killer's owner gains souls equal to the victim's POWER** | the expensive and the faction-defining cards |

Both **accumulate** — they are a pool, not a per-turn allowance — and the HUD
shows the pool and the income separately, exactly as the reference does
(`4 gold`, `INCOME THIS TURN 4`).

**The two resources are a self-balancing pair.** Gold rewards the player who
invests in economy and holds a line; souls reward the player who is winning
fights. A turtle accumulates gold and cannot reach souls; an aggressor
accumulates souls and starves for gold. Neither plan wins alone.

**POWER** is an authored field per card, roughly its total cost, and it is what
the soul award is measured in. Killing a 6-cost character pays six souls;
killing a 1-cost token pays one. **Trading down is punished automatically.**

### 5.3 A character

| stat | range | notes |
|---|---|---|
| `MELEE` | 0–6 | may be 0. Only reaches from your **front** column |
| `RANGED` | 0–5 | may be 0. Reaches from **either** column |
| `HP` | 1–12 | current and max; damage persists between rounds |
| `SHIELD` | 0–4 | **refills to max at the start of every round.** Absorbs that much damage, and **blocks ranged fire from passing into the rear cell behind it** |
| `GOLD` | 0–3 | added at end of round if the character survived |
| `POWER` | 1–10 | what a kill of it is worth in souls |
| abilities | 0..n | keywords and card-specific text |

A character may have both `MELEE` and `RANGED`. A character may have none of
either — a pure economy or support piece.

**Every card carries TWO stat blocks — FRONT and REAR — and the same art.**
This is the mechanical heart of the game. The same card is a different
character depending on where you put it:

```
  ARCHER            cost 3 gold        POWER 3
  FRONT   melee 1   ranged 2   hp 3   shield 0   gold 0
  REAR    melee 0   ranged 3   hp 3   shield 0   gold 0
          (+1 more against their front column, by the rear bonus)

  QUARRYMAN         cost 2 gold        POWER 2
  FRONT   melee 2   ranged 0   hp 5   shield 1   gold 0
  REAR    melee 0   ranged 0   hp 5   shield 0   gold 2
```

HP is normally the same in both blocks; attack, shield and gold are where the
placement decision lives. **Gold is a rear ability that costs you the attack**
— the brief's own rule, and it is what makes the back column a real choice
rather than a safe one.

### 5.4 Targeting

**MELEE** — an attacker in your **front** column, row R. Automatic, no choice:

1. Enemy **front** column, row R — if occupied, hit it.
2. Else enemy **rear** column, row R — if occupied, hit it. *(The gap punish:
   an empty front cell lets the melee through to whatever is behind it.)*
3. Else the **enemy player**.

A melee character in your **rear** column cannot attack at all. That is why
its rear block usually says something else.

**RANGED** — an attacker in **either** of your columns, row R:

- The **enemy front** cell in row R is always a legal target.
- The **enemy rear** cell in row R is legal **only if** the enemy front cell in
  that row is empty **or** the character standing there has **no shield**.
  *(A shield is a wall; an unshielded body is not.)*
- If both enemy cells in row R are empty, the shot hits the **enemy player**.
- **THE REAR BONUS: +1 damage** if the shooter is in **your rear column** and
  the target is in **their front column**. Archers belong behind the line.

**STANCE** — by default a ranged character shoots the enemy **front** cell in
its row. During your turn you may set any of your ranged characters to
**SNIPE**, which targets the enemy **rear** cell instead. Setting a stance is
free and unlimited; it is a decision, not a resource. SNIPE is **greyed** when
the lane's front cell holds a shielded character — §47's rule, and it is a
fact rather than a guess, so it greys rather than failing at resolution.

### 5.5 Placement

**You choose a column, not a cell.** A played character goes into the
**topmost empty cell** of the chosen column.

**Deaths do not compact.** A character that dies leaves its cell empty, and
that hole is then the topmost empty cell and is what fills next. So the board
develops holes, and the holes are where your reinforcements land — which is
rarely where you want them.

**SWAPS — two a turn, four characters moved.** A swap exchanges the contents of
**any two of your own cells**, and an **empty cell is legal content**, so a
plain move is a swap with a hole and costs one of the two.

A swap across columns **changes which stat block is live**: an Archer moved
front-to-rear stops meleeing and starts shooting for 3 (+1). **This is the main
lever the player has over a board they could not place precisely**, and it is
where most of the skill sits.

A swap is **free in resources** and instantaneous — no summoning sickness, no
lost turn. The limit of two is the cost.

### 5.6 Death, souls and the player

- Damage is applied to **SHIELD first**, then HP. Shield refills to max at the
  **start of every round**, so chip damage into a shield is wasted and a burst
  that exceeds it is not. *(This is what makes `PIERCE` and big single hits
  matter, and it is the single most important balance dial in the game.)*
- A character at **0 HP dies** during the casualty phase, after the whole
  combat phase has resolved. Its cell empties.
- **The killer's owner gains souls = the victim's POWER.** Where two attacks
  from different sources kill one character, the soul award goes to whichever
  resolved first in the row order; a simultaneous kill in the same row splits
  down, remainder to the defender. *(Pinned here so it is never a surprise.)*
- **Player HP**: 20 to start. A player loses HP only from damage that no
  character in that lane absorbed — melee into a doubly-empty lane, or a shot
  into one. **Direct damage to a player is the win condition and the only
  clock in the game.**
- A player at **0 HP loses**. Both at 0 in the same combat phase is a **draw**
  — combat is simultaneous, and inventing a tiebreak would be inventing a rule
  the fiction does not have.

### 5.7 The clock

**There is no turn timer and no deck-out.** A 4.77 MHz machine is not a place
to put a shot clock, and a network turn timer would have to be agreed across a
3,741-byte-a-second cable. The clock is the **empty lane**: a board that
deadlocks in every row generates no damage and no souls, and the base income
keeps both players playing, so a stalled board is broken by whoever builds the
better economy. If playtesting finds real deadlocks, the valve is a **round
counter that raises base income** — that is a one-line change and it is
deliberately not in revision 1.

## 6. The phases — and every option, at every point

A **ROUND** is: upkeep, first player's turn, second player's turn, combat,
casualties, spoils, victory check. A **MATCH** is rounds until somebody is at
zero.

### 6.0 MATCH SETUP (once)

1. Both decks are validated (§8.3) and shuffled.
2. **The first player is chosen at random.** In a network match the seed is
   exchanged once (§11.4) so both machines agree; in a campaign match it comes
   from `OSAPI_RAND` seeded from `OSAPI_GET_TICKS`.
3. Both players draw an opening hand of **4**.
4. **The second player is compensated**: **+2 gold and +1 soul**, granted
   before their first turn. *(A starting value. §12 is how it gets tuned.)*
5. Both players start at **20 HP** and **4 gold, 0 souls**.

> **Mulligan is an open question** (§16). A single "redraw your opening hand
> once" is one line of code and a large balance lever.

### 6.1 PHASE 1 — UPKEEP (automatic, ~0.5s, animated)

No player input. In order:

1. Every character's **SHIELD refills to its maximum**. Animated as a brief
   ring on each shielded character — cheap, and it makes the mechanic visible.
2. Status effects tick (poison, marks expiring, timers).
3. Each player **draws 1 card**. Hand limit **7**; a draw over the limit is
   discarded face-up with a toast. *(An empty deck draws nothing and is not a
   loss — see §5.7.)*
4. **Base income is paid**: +2 gold each.

### 6.2 PHASE 2 and 3 — THE TURNS (interactive, untimed)

First player, then second player. The non-active player's board is fully
visible and their hand is not. **Everything below is available at every moment
of your turn, in any order, any number of times unless a limit is stated.**

| action | cost | limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Play a character** to FRONT or REAR | the card's gold and/or souls | while resources and cells last |
| **Swap** two of your cells | free | **2 a turn** |
| **Set a ranged character's STANCE** (Front / Snipe) | free | unlimited |
| **Activate a character's ABILITY** | the ability's cost | once per character per round |
| **Inspect** any card or character | free | unlimited |
| **Concede** | — | ends the match |
| **End Turn** | — | once, and it is the only way out |

**Playing a character.** Pick the card in hand, then pick **FRONT** or **REAR**.
The game shows the destination cell (the topmost empty one in that column)
highlighted before you commit, and shows the **stat block that will be live**
there — so the front/rear decision is made against the numbers, never from
memory. **Refusals are stated, not silent** (§47):

- *not enough gold* / *not enough souls* — the card greys in hand with the
  shortfall in its corner;
- *that column is full* — the column button greys;
- *both columns full* — the card greys.

**A played character does not attack this round.** It was not on the board when
the round began. *(Alternative under discussion in §16: it does. This is a very
large tempo dial.)*

**Swapping.** Click a cell, then a second cell of yours. Both may hold a
character, or one may be empty. The counter reads **SWAPS 2/2** and counts
down; at 0 the cells stop responding to a swap gesture and the counter greys.
**A swap is committed immediately and cannot be undone** — a swap-back costs
the second swap, which is the point.

**Stance.** A ranged character shows a small arrow badge: forward for FRONT,
angled for SNIPE. Clicking it toggles. Greyed when the lane is walled by a
shield.

**Abilities.** A character with an ACTIVE ability shows a badge; clicking it
spends the cost and resolves at once. Passive and end-of-round abilities have
no badge and are listed on the inspect card.

**Inspect.** Hovering a card in hand plays its idle animation (the 23rd
animated feature) and shows both stat blocks. Right-clicking anything on the
board opens its full card — `OSAPI_WM_ONRCLICK` is the slot, and it is the
right gesture because it never competes with the left-click actions.

**End Turn** is a button, and it **confirms when you have resources you could
still have spent** — one alert, and a setting to stop asking.

### 6.3 PHASE 4 — COMBAT (animated, ≤1s a row)

No input except **skip** (any key or click, if `Fast Combat` is off). Rows 0 to
4, in order. For each row:

1. Compute **both** sides' attacks from the state at the start of this row.
2. Play the animation: melee lunges, arrows fly, impacts land, damage numbers
   rise.
3. Apply all of that row's damage **together**.

Damage order within a target: **shield, then HP**. A character reduced to 0 HP
is **marked** dead but stays on the board until the casualty phase, so it still
deals its own damage this round — **simultaneity is total**.

### 6.4 PHASE 5 — CASUALTIES (animated, ~1s)

Marked characters die together. Each plays a death animation, its cell empties,
and **the soul award floats to the killer's HUD** — which is the moment the
economy of the game is legible, so it is worth the second.

**On-death abilities resolve here**, in row order then column order, and a
character raised by another's death arrives *after* all deaths are resolved, so
no chain can run inside a single casualty phase.

### 6.5 PHASE 6 — SPOILS / END OF ROUND (animated, in sequence)

The brief: *"at the end of round, when characters are taking their end of round
action, they can play in sequence."* So they do — one at a time, top-left to
bottom-right, each with a small animation, and the wheel's whole credit goes to
whichever is acting.

In order, per player, first player first:

1. **Gold generation.** Each surviving character's `GOLD` is added, with a coin
   animation on the character and a running total in the HUD.
2. **Regeneration and healing.**
3. **End-of-round keyword abilities** (`CARRION`, `TITHE`, decay, etc.).
4. The **income figure** for the next round is banked and shown.

**A gold character must survive combat to pay.** Spoils are after casualties,
deliberately — it puts economy pieces under real pressure and makes killing
them a plan rather than a side effect.

### 6.6 PHASE 7 — VICTORY CHECK

A player at 0 HP has lost. Both at 0 is a draw. Otherwise the round counter
advances and §6.1 runs again.

**Victory screen**: the result, the round count, damage dealt, souls earned,
characters killed and lost — and in campaign, the **reward** (§10.3).

### 6.7 OUT-OF-TURN — what the waiting player may do

**Nothing that changes state**, and everything that does not:

- inspect any card on the board (right-click);
- read their own hand;
- open the log (§6.8);
- concede;
- in a network match, see a **turn indicator** and a **link state** badge.

There are no instants, no interrupts and no reactions. **This is a deliberate
simplification and it is worth naming**: instants would mean the network
protocol has to interleave two players' inputs inside one phase, which turns a
simple turn-swap (§11.4) into a real synchronisation problem on a cable that
moves 3,741 bytes a second.

### 6.8 THE LOG

A scrollable list of what happened, one line an event, cleared per match.
`os88ui.inc`'s scroll bar is the control. It is cheap, it is what a turn-based
game is debugged with, and in a network match it is what tells a player why
something they did not see happened.

### 6.9 SETTINGS

Per-machine, stored in `SYSTEM/APPDATA/<STEM>.CFG`:

| setting | arms | default |
|---|---|---|
| **Detail** | Flat / Banded / Rich | from `OSAPI_CPU_INFO` and the adapter |
| **Fast Combat** | Off / On | Off |
| **Animations** | Full / Reduced / Off | Full |
| **Confirm End Turn** | On / Off | On |
| **Sound** | On / Off | On |
| **AI Difficulty** | Recruit / Veteran / Warlord | Veteran |

**Animations: Off** is not a cosmetic arm — it is the honest answer for a
machine that cannot hold the rate, and it is what makes the game playable on
whatever the slowest supported configuration turns out to be.

## 7. The factions and the cards

Three factions at launch. **The implementation is a table and a faction id, not
three code paths** — a fourth faction must be a data change plus its art, and
anything a faction needs that the table cannot express is a keyword (§7.5),
which every faction may then use.

### 7.1 LINE — the shield-and-gold faction

**Identity**: holds the front, denies the lane, out-economies. The faction that
makes the *board* do the work.

| | |
|---|---|
| plays to | strong **FRONT** blocks with shields; **REAR** gold engines |
| wins by | surviving to a bigger income than the opponent's, then converting |
| loses to | burst that exceeds a shield in one hit, and to `PIERCE` |
| resource skew | gold-heavy, soul-poor (it kills slowly) |

Keywords: **GUARD** (while this is in your front column, the character directly
behind it cannot be reached by the melee gap-punish), **TITHE n** (end of
round: +n gold, rear only), **BULWARK** (shield does not fall below 1 even
after a hit).

### 7.2 REACH — the ranged faction

**Identity**: precision. Shoots what it chooses, ignores walls, punishes a
board that has been arranged badly.

| | |
|---|---|
| plays to | **REAR** shooters taking the +1, thin front bodies to hold the lane |
| wins by | killing the right character rather than the nearest one |
| loses to | pressure — it is fragile and its front line is a formality |
| resource skew | soul-heavy (it trades well), gold-poor |

Keywords: **PIERCE n** (ignores n of the target's shield — *and* may SNIPE past
a shield of n or less), **VOLLEY** (also deals 1 to the characters in the rows
above and below the target), **MARK** (a marked enemy takes +1 from every
source until end of round).

### 7.3 SWARM — the many, and the dead

**Identity**: bodies. Individually worthless, collectively a problem, and it
turns its own casualties into resources.

| | |
|---|---|
| plays to | filling all ten cells with cheap characters |
| wins by | out-numbering, and by an economy the other two cannot have |
| loses to | `VOLLEY` and anything that kills several at once |
| resource skew | **soul-powered** — several cards cost souls and no gold |

Keywords: **HARVEST** (you gain souls when **your own** characters die, at half
POWER, rounded up — this is the faction's whole engine and its balance dial),
**RAISE n** (on death, put an n-POWER token in this cell), **RECKLESS** (deals
its melee damage but takes 1 itself), **CARRION** (end of round: +1 gold for
each enemy that died this round).

### 7.4 Why these three interlock

| | beats | because |
|---|---|---|
| LINE | SWARM | shields absorb a swarm's small hits entirely, and a swarm's POWER is low so its deaths pay LINE badly — but LINE does not need souls |
| REACH | LINE | `PIERCE` and burst walk through shields, and `SNIPE` kills the gold engines behind them |
| SWARM | REACH | REACH is fragile and cannot kill ten bodies fast enough; every SWARM death pays SWARM |

**That is rock-paper-scissors at the archetype level, and it is on purpose**:
it gives the campaign somewhere to go (§10.2 gates factions behind opponents
that punish your current one) and it gives deck-building a reason beyond
"play the good cards."

### 7.5 Card types — and the fork

Revision 1 proposes **characters only**, because the brief describes only
characters and because every character already carries two stat blocks, which
is a lot of design space. Two additions are on the table and **the owner should
decide before the card list is written**, since they change the cost curve of
every card:

- **ORDERS** — one-shot effects played from hand (a burst of damage, a forced
  swap of an *enemy's* two cells, a shield strip, a resurrection). They make
  the game less deterministic and give a losing board an out. They also make
  the AI much harder and the network log much longer.
- **RELICS** — a permanent attached to one of your characters (+2 HP, grant
  `PIERCE 1`, convert its gold into souls). Cheap to implement; they let a
  faction's identity be *applied* to a character rather than printed on it.

**Recommendation: characters in revision 1, ORDERS in revision 2, RELICS
probably never** — relics add a second object on every cell and the cell is
already carrying a sprite, an HP bar, a shield ring and a stance badge on a
64×80 tile.

### 7.6 The card list — the shape of it

**16 characters a faction, 48 in all.** A faction's curve:

| cost | count | role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | filler bodies; a rear 1-gold engine |
| 2 | 3 | the workhorses — one front blocker, one shooter, one economy |
| 3 | 4 | the faction's identity cards; where keywords first appear |
| 4 | 3 | swing pieces |
| 5 | 2 | expensive and board-defining |
| 6+ | 2 | one gold-and-soul finisher, one soul-only |

**The full 48-card list is revision 2's work** and it should not be written
until §12's balance harness exists, because a card list written without one is
a list of guesses. §12.1 says what has to be in place first.

## 8. Deck building

### 8.1 The collection

Cards are **owned**, and ownership is earned in the campaign (§10.3). A fresh
install grants a **starter deck per faction** — the 20 cards that make each
faction playable — so a new player can duel, network or AI, immediately, and
the campaign is a *widening* rather than an unlock gate.

The collection lives in `SYSTEM/APPDATA/<STEM>.SAV` beside the campaign
progress (§10.4).

### 8.2 The builder

A screen of its own, reachable from the main menu and from the pre-match
screen. Left: the collection, filtered by faction, sorted by cost. Right: the
deck, grouped by cost with a cost curve drawn as a small histogram — which is
the one piece of UI that teaches deck-building without a tutorial.

Clicking adds, right-clicking removes, hovering shows the full card with both
stat blocks and its idle animation.

### 8.3 The rules

| | |
|---|---|
| deck size | **exactly 20** |
| faction | **one faction per deck**; no neutral cards in revision 1 |
| copies | **at most 2** of any card |
| decks saved | up to **8**, named |

A deck that does not satisfy these cannot be selected, and the reason is on
screen — *"18 of 20"*, *"3 copies of Quarryman"* — rather than a refusal at
match start.

**"One faction per deck" is worth defending**: it is what makes the three
identities legible, it halves the art that must be resident (§2.5), and it is
what lets the pre-match screen load exactly two faction parts.

## 9. The AI

### 9.1 What it has to be

An AI turn is: enumerate the legal actions, score each, take the best, repeat
until nothing is worth doing, then end turn. **On a 4.77 MHz 8088 the whole
turn must fit in about a second of thinking**, or the game feels broken.

### 9.2 The branching, counted

| action | count |
|---|---|
| play a card | hand (≤7) × 2 columns = **≤14** |
| swap | 10 cells choose 2 = **45**, twice |
| stance | ≤10 |

So a single ply is **≤70 candidate actions**, and a turn is a handful of plies.
That is affordable **if the evaluation is cheap** — and it is, because it is
integer arithmetic over a 20-cell board.

### 9.3 The evaluation

A board score, from the AI's point of view:

```
score =   Wp * (my player HP - their player HP)
        + Wb * (my board POWER - their board POWER)
        + Wt * (damage I will deal next combat - damage they will deal)
        + Wk * (POWER I will kill next combat - POWER I will lose)
        + Wr * (my gold income - their gold income)
        + Wh * (cards in my hand I can afford)
        + Wl * (lanes where I am unopposed)
```

**The last two terms are what make it play the actual game**: an unopposed lane
is direct damage to a player, and a hand of cards you cannot afford is a
mis-spent economy.

**The one-ply lookahead is the combat simulator itself** — the same code that
resolves §6.3, run on a scratch copy of the board with no animation. That is
the single most valuable structural decision in the AI: **the simulator and the
game are one body of code**, so the AI cannot be wrong about the rules, which
is the failure mode that makes game AI feel cheap.

### 9.4 Difficulty

Not by cheating. Three honest arms:

| arm | how |
|---|---|
| **Recruit** | evaluation terms `Wt`, `Wk` and `Wl` zeroed — it plays for board presence and ignores the fight; and it takes the *second* best action 1 time in 3 |
| **Veteran** | the full evaluation, one ply |
| **Warlord** | the full evaluation, plus it considers its **swaps before its plays** (which is the stronger ordering and the one humans miss), and it looks one further round ahead on the resource curve |

**A campaign opponent also has a personality**: a weight set and a deck, both in
the same table, so "the SWARM general who over-commits" is data.

### 9.5 Where it runs

**On the worker task**, not in the click handler. A one-second think in a
`W_ONCLICK` is one second with the gfx lock held, and §7 is unambiguous about
what that costs: the pointer stops, the board stops, and the machine reads as
frozen. The worker thinks, posts `OSAPI_WM_WAKE`, and the UI task applies the
move on the wake — which is the same shape `apps/ftpd` uses for a socket and
is the only shape §20.6 rule 7 permits.

**The AI animates while it thinks.** The wheel keeps turning on the UI task,
so an AI turn looks like a player taking their time rather than a hang. That
falls straight out of putting the think on the worker and is worth stating
because getting it wrong is the difference between a game and a stall.

## 10. The campaign

### 10.1 The shape

A **map** of nodes joined by paths. Each node is a duel against a named AI
opponent with a fixed deck and personality. Beating a node opens its
successors and pays a reward. The map is a data file (`MAP.DAT`), so a second
campaign is content and not code.

**Three regions, one per faction's homeland**, roughly 8–10 nodes each, plus a
final node. Starting region chosen by the player.

### 10.2 What a node is

| field | |
|---|---|
| name, portrait, one line of flavour | |
| opponent faction and deck | |
| AI weight set and difficulty | |
| reward | |
| unlocks | the node ids it opens |
| **a modifier** (optional) | a rule that applies to this duel only |

**Modifiers are the campaign's whole texture** and they are cheap, because
every one of them is a flag the rules engine already reads: *"the enemy starts
with a free 2-cost character in each front cell"*, *"lane 2 is impassable"*,
*"you start with 10 gold and 10 HP"*, *"shields do not regenerate"*. Twenty
nodes with twenty modifiers is a different game twenty times over for the cost
of a table.

### 10.3 Rewards

- a **specific card** (how a faction's identity cards are acquired);
- a **choice of three** (the interesting one — it is a deck-building decision
  rather than a lottery);
- **gold**, spendable in a **shop node** that offers a rotating handful.

**No random packs.** A random pack on a machine with no way to trade is a
frustration generator, and the choice-of-three is strictly better design for
the same implementation cost.

### 10.4 The save

`SYSTEM/APPDATA/<STEM>.SAV`: a version word, the collection as a count per
card id, the decks, the node states, gold, and a checksum. Written with
`OSAPI_FILE_WRITE` at the end of a duel and at every deck edit — **never
mid-match**, because a file write is UI-task context and seconds of `int 13h`.

A save whose version or checksum does not match is **reported and kept**, not
silently reset. §18.94's discipline: carry the evidence out.

## 11. Multiplayer

### 11.1 The three transports, and their honest status

| transport | status | what it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **hot-seat** — two players, one machine | **free** | a "pass the machine" screen that hides the hand |
| **Ethernet** — two os8088 machines, each with a NIC | **buildable today** | `apps/os88sock.inc` + `drivers/net/netpkg.inc`; `ETHER.DRV` publishes `NETV_LISTEN` and `NETV_ACCEPT` |
| **direct cable** — null modem, serial or parallel | **needs new work, and it is a driver** | see §11.5 |

**Hot-seat is not a consolation prize.** It is the mode that always works, it
is how the game gets playtested, and it is what the balance harness (§12) is
built on. It ships in wave 1.

### 11.2 Ethernet, concretely

`os88sock.inc`'s `net_find` answers which class is serving sockets and sets
`NET_CLASS`; every verb is **non-blocking** by contract, because a blocking
socket call would be a hang with the gfx lock held.

```
HOST:  NETV_LISTEN (CX = port)  -> a listening handle
       NETV_ACCEPT              -> polled from the worker until a handle arrives
JOIN:  NETV_OPEN  (ES:SI = a NUL host name or dotted quad)
       NETV_STATUS              -> polled until NSK_UP
```

**The trap the header states twice**: a zero-length `NETV_RECV` is **not**
end-of-stream. The end is `NSK_CLOSING` with nothing readable. A message that
arrives in two writes would otherwise be truncated at the gap.

The socket is driven entirely from the **worker**, which is why every verb is
on §20.6 rule 7's allowlist; the worker stages received messages into a ring in
the package's own segment and posts `OSAPI_WM_WAKE`, and the UI task applies
them. **The staging buffer must be in the package's own segment** — §77.10's
lesson: `OSAPI_DRV_CALL` puts the *caller's* segment in ES, so a buffer in a
heap claim is read out of the package image instead, and it presents as memory
corruption rather than as a wrong segment register.

### 11.3 The protocol

Turn-based, so this is easy and should be kept easy. **Action-replication, not
state-replication**: each side owns its own deck, its own hand and its own RNG,
and sends only what the other side must know.

```
  HELLO    version, faction, deck hash, a 16-bit seed, player name
  READY    both sides have loaded their faction parts
  PLAY     card id, column
  SWAP     cell a, cell b
  STANCE   cell, stance
  ABILITY  cell, ability index, target
  ENDTURN  followed by a 16-bit CHECKSUM of the whole board state
  CONCEDE
  PING     every ~3 seconds of idle, so a dropped link is noticed
```

Messages are short — a `PLAY` is four bytes — so even a 3,741 byte/second cable
carries a turn instantly. **The bandwidth is irrelevant; the latency and the
liveness are what matter.**

**The checksum on `ENDTURN` is the whole safety net.** Two machines running the
same rules on the same actions must reach the same board. If the checksums
differ, the match stops and says **desync** with the round number — rather than
quietly diverging into two different games, which is the failure mode that
makes networked play untrustworthy. In development the mismatching side also
dumps both boards to a file.

**The first player is decided by the seed in `HELLO`**, exchanged both ways and
combined, so neither side can pick.

### 11.4 Who does what, and when

Only the player whose turn it is sends actions. The other side **applies them
as they arrive and animates them** — so you watch your opponent play, which is
most of what makes a networked turn-based game feel live. `ENDTURN` hands over.

A link that drops mid-match: the receiving side shows **Link lost** with the
round, offers **reconnect** (the protocol resumes from the last agreed
`ENDTURN`, because both sides hold a consistent board at that point) and
**concede**. Reconnect is revision 2; revision 1 reports and ends the match.

### 11.5 The direct cable — what it actually costs

**This is the one part of the brief the OS does not already do, and it should
be planned honestly rather than assumed.**

What exists: `drivers/net/lplink.inc`, a fully-interlocked four-phase nibble
transport over a LapLink parallel cable, measured at **3,741 bytes/second** on
real hardware, with every deadline counted **in system ticks** because the two
ends are not alike. It is `%include`d by both `NET.DRV` and the field
diagnostic, so there is exactly one implementation.

What does not exist:

1. **An os8088-to-os8088 peer.** `NET.DRV` is written with os8088 as the
   **master** and a DOS program as the slave (`NET-PLAN` §1.3), and its socket
   verbs forward to a TCP stack *on the DOS side*. Two os8088 machines have no
   master and no slave, and neither has a stack to forward to.
2. **Any serial transport at all.** The mouse owns a COM port for input; there
   is no UART driver, no framing, no flow control. A serial null modem is a
   **new driver from nothing**, and it has a hazard the parallel one does not:
   the mouse is on a COM port, so port arbitration is a real design question
   rather than a detail.

Three candidate designs, and the plan recommends the third:

- **(a) Extend `NET.DRV` with a peer mode.** Cheapest in new code; worst in
  ownership. It makes a driver whose subject is *"a volume served by a DOS
  machine"* also the owner of a game's transport, and it makes every user of
  the cable pay resident bytes for it.
- **(b) Do it inside the package.** Refused. A package banging parallel ports
  directly makes two owners of one port, and `DOS-CABLE-NET-PLAN` §2 already
  refused the same shape for the same reason.
- **(c) `LINK.DRV` — a new driver, one subject: a cable between two os8088
  machines.** It `%include`s `lplink.inc` for the parallel arm exactly as the
  diagnostic does, adds a serial arm behind the same framing, publishes
  `NETV_*`-shaped verbs so **`os88sock.inc` finds it and the game's networking
  code does not change one line**, and is not mounted by default (`DRVR_WANT`
  ships 0, §51.3 — and §62.1's particular reason applies doubly: finding a
  cable means *writing to ports*, and a port with a printer on it is a real
  machine).

  Its symmetry problem is solved the way the hello already solves it: both
  ends probe, and **the end that answers first is the slave** — or, more simply,
  the player who chose **Host** is the master and the one who chose **Join** is
  the slave, which is a decision the *game* has already made before the driver
  is asked.

**Staging.** `LINK.DRV` is a wave of its own and it is **not** in the game's
critical path: the game is complete and shippable with hot-seat, AI, campaign
and Ethernet. Building the cable transport first would put the hardest,
least-testable component (no emulator here has a null modem between two guests)
in front of everything the player actually sees.

### 11.6 What can and cannot be tested

`docs/TESTING.md`'s list is binding, and networking is on it: **MartyPC has no
NIC of any kind**, so every Ethernet row boots QEMU, as `tests/ethernet.py`
already does. A **cable between two guests** is on nobody's list — the
parallel link's own gate is `tests/lptlink/linksim.py`, a host-side model of
both ends over one microsecond clock, and it **caught three timing bugs before
any hardware ran**. `LINK.DRV` gets the same instrument, and the same warning
attached: the simulator found the three that a fast host and a slow guest would
each have hidden from the other, and the *field* found six it could not.

## 12. Balance — how it gets iterated

**Balance is named in the brief as a large factor, so it gets a mechanism
rather than a promise.**

### 12.1 The harness comes before the card list

`tools/duelsim.py` — a **host-side Python reference implementation of the
rules**, in `tools/weavesim.py`'s and `tools/htmsim.py`'s shape. It:

- plays AI-vs-AI matches at thousands a second, with no machine involved;
- reads the **same card table** the package compiles in, so the two cannot
  drift — the table is a generated include, and the simulator and the build
  read one file;
- reports win rate by faction, by deck, by first/second player, match length
  distribution, and per-card presence in winning decks;
- and is `--selfcheck`ed in the build the way `weavesim.py` is.

**And it is the second reader of the rules**, which is the other half of its
value: the assembly and the Python must agree, and a `soak` row that plays one
scripted match on the machine and diffs the log against the simulator's is what
keeps them agreeing.

### 12.2 The dials, in the order they should be reached for

1. **Second-player compensation** (+2 gold, +1 soul). The single most
   measurable number in the game: the harness reports first-player win rate
   directly, and the target is 50% ± 2.
2. **Shield regeneration.** Full regen every round is a big number. If burst
   dominates, partial regen; if attrition dominates, full.
3. **The soul award rate** (currently POWER × 1). Halving it slows the whole
   game; `HARVEST`'s half-rate is SWARM's engine and is the most fragile
   number in the design.
4. **The rear ranged bonus** (+1). Small, but it is the entire reason the back
   column is not just an economy zone.
5. **Base income** (+2). Raises the whole curve; reach for it last.
6. **Individual card numbers.** Last, always — a card nerfed to fix a systemic
   problem is a card that has to be un-nerfed later.

### 12.3 What good looks like

| | target |
|---|---|
| first-player win rate | 50% ± 2 |
| faction win rates, mirror-excluded | 33% ± 4 each |
| match length | median 9–14 rounds; under 6 or over 25 is a bug |
| cards never played in a winning deck | **zero** — a card nobody plays is a card that needs changing, not a card that is weak |

## 13. Where it lives, and what the build owes

### 13.1 Files

```
apps/<stem>/
  <stem>.asm      header, icon, entry, window callbacks, worker, the .bss
  xxlay.inc       layout from the live content box; the cell table
  xxrend.inc      the band composer, the pacing wheel, the HUD
  xxspr.inc       the sprite bank: load, cut to the cell, index
  xxrule.inc      THE RULES ENGINE - targeting, combat, deaths, spoils
  xxcard.inc      the card table (GENERATED from the same source as duelsim.py)
  xxai.inc        evaluation and action enumeration
  xxnet.inc       the protocol, over os88sock.inc
  xxcamp.inc      the map, the nodes, the save
  xxmenu.inc      the animated front menu and its buttons
  xxdeck.inc      the deck builder
  art/            the PNG masters
tools/os88cards.py   art -> the shipped banks
tools/duelsim.py     the reference implementation and the balance harness
```

**`xxrule.inc` is used by the game, by the AI and by the network checksum, and
it is the only place the rules exist.** That is the load-bearing structural
decision in the whole package.

### 13.2 The build

- `make duel` builds the package; `make dueldisk` builds the floppy **in all
  four geometries** (§19's rule reaches the on-demand application disks too —
  1.44MB, 1.2MB, 720KB and the binding 360KB).
- 86Box profiles: `xt-duel` (the 4.77 MHz XT the performance claims are about),
  `286-duel`, `386-duel`. The XT one is the point, not the postponement —
  `xt-paccman`'s precedent.
- **`tests/unit/t_livefull.py` is in the FAST tier and sweeps `apps/`**: a new
  package directory **fails `make`** until it is on the live media or written
  into that file's exemptions with a reason. Ours goes on the live media.
- `$(SMALLOMIT)` gets the stem, with the reason (§2.6), and `soak -k 'smallreq'`
  reads the built floppy rather than the variable.
- `tests/movable.txt` / `t_movable.py`: the region and the worker both declare
  (§4.3).
- `docs/INDEX.md` is **generated** and checked by every `make` — adding a
  package means regenerating it.
- SPEC.md **§97** is written **before** the change, not after (§1's rule).

### 13.3 The tests, by tier

| tier | rows |
|---|---|
| `fast` | the card table's invariants (costs, POWER, both stat blocks present, ≤2-copy legality of every starter deck); `t_livefull`; `duelsim --selfcheck` |
| `full` | nothing — this is one package, and `full` asks only *did you obviously break the OS* |
| `soak` | `duelrules` (a scripted match on the machine, diffed against `duelsim.py`); `duelframe` (the frame budget under MartyPC on all three adapters, windowed and fullscreen — the §2.3 table, asserted); `duelai` (an AI match completes inside a time bound); `duelnet` (two QEMU guests over Ethernet, the `tests/ethernet.py` shape); `duelsave` (a campaign save round-trips) |

**Every row gets a `secs` somebody measured**, and every row is written by
breaking the thing on purpose first and watching it go red —
`docs/WRITING-TESTS.md` §1, and its §13 is sixty incidents' worth of why.

## 15. The refusals, recorded now

Things this plan deliberately does **not** do, so they are not re-proposed:

- **`gfx_blit4` for anything that animates.** §2.2: 114.7 ms for one 64×80
  sprite on a VGA.
- **An `fsx` bracket with a foreign mode.** §2.6: two art pipelines, no cursor,
  no event ladder, for a turn-based game that wants all three.
- **Erase-then-draw.** §3.2: the screen saver shipped it, the field called it a
  strobe, and one opaque band is both cheaper and correct.
- **Instants, interrupts and reactions.** §6.7: they turn a turn-swap into a
  synchronisation problem on a 3,741 byte/second cable.
- **Random card packs.** §10.3: choice-of-three is the same code and better
  design.
- **A turn timer.** §5.7.
- **State replication over the wire.** §11.3: actions are four bytes and a
  board is not.
- **Putting the cable transport in the package, or in `NET.DRV`.** §11.5.

## 16. Open questions — the owner's to answer

1. **The name**, and the three faction names (§1). Everything else is a
   find-and-replace once this is settled.
2. **The brief's last sentence is cut off** — *"Characters "* — after the stat
   list. What was it going to say?
3. **CGA** (§2.4): 200 rows will not hold five rows of characters, a hand and a
   HUD at any sprite size worth drawing. Tiny characters, a scrolling board, or
   an honest refusal?
4. **Does a character played this turn attack in this round's combat?** (§6.2.)
   A very large tempo dial, and it should be decided before any card is costed.
5. **Orders and relics** (§7.5): characters only in revision 1?
6. **Mulligan** (§6.0): one free redraw of the opening hand?
7. **Souls from your own dead** — is `HARVEST` a SWARM keyword, or a universal
   rule at a lower rate? (Universal gives a losing player an out; a keyword
   gives SWARM its identity. It cannot really be both.)
8. **Melee into an empty front cell**: this plan says it reaches the rear cell
   behind it (§5.4). The alternative is that it hits the player. The first
   makes rear placement dangerous; the second makes an empty lane a bigger
   emergency.
9. **Deck size 20 and hand limit 7** — starting values only.
10. **Sound**: PC speaker tones for the UI and combat, and FM stingers when
    `SOUND.DRV` is present? The brief does not mention it and it is real work.
11. **Board orientation**: the reference is isometric. A **flat, straight-on**
    board is dramatically cheaper (a cell is a rectangle, a band is a band, and
    the cell grid is a multiple of 8 for free). An isometric board means
    overlapping cells, a draw order, and bands that are not rectangles.
    **This plan assumes flat**, and it is the single largest art-direction
    question in the document.
